# Writing clean the first time

This guide is for whoever writes a new text: `draft`, `summarise` and `compare` read it before they write, and any other skill that hands its prose to `humanise` may do the same. It is the pattern catalogue (`catalogue.md`) turned round, so each entry says how to write the sentence well in the first place. The numbers match the catalogue and the scanner (`S01` to `S59`), so a hit from `humanise` points straight back to the entry that would have prevented it.

`humanise` still runs afterwards. When the text was written to this guide it usually passes as written, and `humanise` hands it back unchanged. When it does not, `humanise` rewrites only the paragraphs that failed. Every rewrite costs a second pass and risks losing a point, so getting a sentence right here is cheaper than getting it fixed there.

When a pattern changes in `catalogue.md`, its entry here changes in the same edit. Every catalogue number from 1 to 59 has exactly one entry below.

## Before the first sentence

- **Know the point and the order.** Settle the claims, figures, dates, conditions and caveats the text must carry, put them in the order the reader needs, and know where the text ends.
- **Ask one question of every sentence:** what does the reader have to work out before it means anything? The answer should be nothing. A placeholder where a name exists, a metaphor where a mechanism exists, or a scene where a subject exists each make the reader do work the writer skipped.
- **Quotations are out of scope.** Words between quotation marks are the source's and stay exactly as the source wrote them, dashes and all. Titles, proper names and a phrase the text is discussing are left alone too.
- **Plain writing.** Start where the reader is and assume they share none of the source's internal terms. Use words a person would say aloud to one other person, words that make a picture, and active verbs. Every sentence supports the point or goes.

## Two hard rules

- **§9: state the claim directly.** Write what something is or does. Never set it against a foil nobody proposed: no `not X but Y`, `not just`, `not merely`, `not only ... but also`, `X, not Y`, `it's not X, it's Y`, `rather than` or `instead of` used to pin a claim against a foil, an either/or set up for rhythm, the two-sentence form `X is not A. It is B.`, or a clipped tail such as `, no guessing`. When the excluded reading carries real information, give it a sentence of its own.
- **§14: no dashes in your own words.** No em dash, en dash, other dash character or spaced double hyphen. Use a full stop, a comma, a colon or brackets, or rebuild the sentence. A dash inside a quotation stays.

## Content

- **§1 Inflated significance.** Report the event and its date. Give a consequence only when a source states it, and write it as a consequence with its source. Avoid `marks a turning point`, `reflects a broader shift`, `a lasting legacy`, `plays a key role`, `sets the stage for`.
- **§2 Notability by list.** To show that a person or organisation matters, report one thing they said or did, with its source. A list of outlets, awards or follower counts proves nothing.
- **§3 Trailing -ing commentary.** End the sentence when the fact ends. A clause like `, reflecting ...`, `, highlighting ...`, `, ensuring ...` or `, signalling ...` is either cut or becomes its own sentence with a source and a subject who did the interpreting.
- **§4 Promotional wording.** Give the fact a selling adjective stands in for: the size, the date, what happens there. Avoid `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `rich heritage`, `cutting-edge`, `stunning`, `vibrant`, `boasting`, `charming`.
- **§5 Vague attribution.** Name the source of every opinion from the documents. With none, the view is yours: say so or leave it out. Avoid `experts believe`, `observers note`, `critics say`, `industry reports suggest`.
- **§6 The formulaic challenges section.** Name each difficulty with its evidence, or leave difficulties out. Never close on a dismissal like `despite these challenges, it continues to thrive`.

## Language

- **§7 Machine vocabulary.** Use the plain word: look at, mix, proof, key, important, detailed, help, gain, show, stress, lively, solid, smooth, use, whole, field, many. Avoid `delve`, `tapestry`, `testament`, `pivotal`, `crucial`, `intricate`, `interplay`, `foster`, `garner`, `showcase`, `underscore`, `vibrant`, `robust`, `seamless`, `leverage`, `holistic`, `realm`, `myriad`, `anchored to` meaning "based on", and `landscape` for anything other than land. Often the sentence is better with the word simply gone.
- **§8 Avoiding "is" and "has".** Use is, are, has and have. Avoid `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `boasts`, `features a`, `represents a`.
- **§9** is a hard rule, above.
- **§10 Reflexive groups of three.** List as many items as the facts support, whether that is one, two or five. Three adjectives or three clauses that restate one idea are padding. A repeated opening that carries three different ideas is a chosen device and may stay.
- **§11 Synonym cycling.** Pick one name for each thing and keep it. The depot stays the depot; it never becomes the facility, the site and the hub in successive sentences.
- **§12 False ranges.** Write `from X to Y` only when X and Y are two ends of a real scale. Otherwise list what is covered.
- **§13 Hidden actors and subjectless fragments.** Say who does what. Write `You do not need an account` in place of `No account required`, and `The app copies your data to the server` in place of `Data is synced`. Passive voice is fine when the actor does not matter to the reader.

## Style

- **§14** is a hard rule, above.
- **§15 Bold scattered through prose.** Leave prose unbolded. Bold only a term the reader must find again, or the one sentence a skimming reader should take away.
- **§16 Lists led by bold labels.** Write a paragraph. Use a list only when the items are parallel and the reader will scan them, and never open each item with a bold label and a colon that the sentence then repeats.
- **§17 Title Case in headings.** Follow the document's heading style. With none, use sentence case.
- **§18 Emoji as decoration.** Use no emoji. If one would carry meaning (a warning, a done mark), write the meaning in words.
- **§19 Quotation marks.** Use one style of quotation mark throughout, straight or curly. Inside a quotation the source's marks stay.

## Communication

- **§20 Chat residue.** The document never speaks to the person who asked for it. Avoid `Here is an overview`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if`, `Certainly!`, `Would you like me to`, `I hope this email finds you well`, `Please don't hesitate to`.
- **§21 Cutoff disclaimers and guessed filler.** When the documents do not hold a fact, say so in one line or leave a placeholder (`[delivery date: not in the documents]`). Never fill the gap with a likely guess, and never write `as of my last update`, `details are limited`, `keeps a low profile` or `likely grew up in`.
- **§22 Flattery of the reader.** Start with the answer. Avoid `Great question`, `You're absolutely right`, `That's an excellent point`.

## Filler and hedging

- **§23 Filler phrases.** Use the short form: to, because, now, if, can, many or the number. Avoid `in order to`, `due to the fact that`, `at this point in time`, `in the event that`, `has the ability to`, `a wide range of`. Start with the point where you would write `it is worth noting that`.
- **§24 Stacked hedges.** Give a claim the one hedge its evidence calls for, or none. Avoid `could potentially`, `might possibly`, `it could perhaps be argued`.
- **§25 Upbeat empty endings.** End on the last concrete fact, or on what happens next and when. Avoid `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `a step in the right direction`.

## Further patterns

- **§26 Hyphenated pairs after the noun.** Hyphenate a compound before its noun (`a high-quality report`) and drop the hyphen after a linking verb (`the report is high quality`).
- **§27 Authority tropes.** Make the point without announcing that it is the deep one. Avoid `the real question is`, `at its core`, `what really matters`, `fundamentally,`, `the heart of the matter`.
- **§28 Signposting and announcements.** Do what you were about to announce. Avoid `let's dive in`, `here's what you need to know`, `let's break this down`, `without further ado`, and the promised simplification: `in plain English`, `put simply`, `in a nutshell`, never in a title.
- **§29 Fragmented headers.** After a heading, start on the content. Never follow a heading with a short line that restates it.
- **§30 Diff-anchored writing.** Describe things as they are now. Outside a changelog, release notes or a migration guide, avoid `was added to replace`, `previously`, `the old approach`, `now uses` meaning "has changed to use".
- **§31 Staccato and manufactured punchlines.** Let sentences carry their reasoning. One short sentence where the point lands is fine; three or more short ones in a row read as engineered drama.
- **§32 Aphorism formulas.** State the concrete claim. Avoid maxims like `X is the language of Y`, `X becomes a trap`, `X is a mirror`.
- **§33 Fake-candid openers.** Start with the answer. Avoid `Honestly?`, `Look,`, `Here's the thing`, `Real talk`.
- **§34 Self-referential summary closers.** Let the last substantive sentence end the text. Avoid `that is the whole point`, `nothing more and nothing less`, `and that is what makes it work`, `at the end of the day`. A causal link or a pointer to more detail is content and may close.
- **§35 Folksy idiom filler.** Say what the effort buys or what the trade-off is. Avoid `earns its keep`, `low-hanging fruit`, `moves the needle`, `the sweet spot`, `no silver bullet`, `boils down to`.
- **§36 Contentless structural headings.** Name the subject in each heading (`Scanner, sorter and loading bay`). Avoid counts and category labels like `The three pieces`, `Key components`, `Important considerations`. Conventional navigation headings (Installation, Requirements, Troubleshooting) are fine.
- **§37 Question-shaped and copula headings.** Use a noun phrase, often a gerund, in sentence case (`Building a rota`). Avoid `What a rota is`, `How it works`, `Why it matters`, and headings that end in a question mark.
- **§38 False time-stakes.** Write a day, an hour or a slice of leisure only when the claim depends on it: a real date, a deadline, a measured duration, an incident time. Avoid `use it on Monday`, `at 2 a.m.`, `save an afternoon`, `overnight`. If any other weekday would do as well, the time is decoration.
- **§39 Uniform enumeration.** When a paragraph covers a set of three or more, group the members into the two or three things the set is saying, name each group in a short sentence, and vary how the members are written. A run of long sentences built on one template (`[X] fell from [A] to [B]`) is a list with the bullets removed. Changing only the verb leaves the template intact. A bulleted list may be uniform.
- **§40 A description that mirrors its paragraph.** In a caption, alt text or figure description, say first what the picture is and how to read it (the kind of chart, what the marks mean), then give every value. Never recite the paragraph beside it in the same order and wording.
- **§41 Conclusions with their joints removed.** Keep the reasoning between findings: what was observed, what was expected, who inferred what, and what a figure is compared against. Put a probability or rate in a form the reader can picture. Mark an untested explanation as a proposal.
- **§42 The staged imperative.** Make the reader's object the subject of a statement (`Every gap in your rota is a shift ...`). Avoid a short command that sets a scene for the next sentence to reveal (`Open your rota.`, `Picture a loading bay.`). Real instructions in a procedure or checklist are fine.
- **§43 The unmasking formula.** State a reclassification plainly and give its evidence in the next sentence. Avoid `X wearing a Y costume`, `X dressed up as Y`, `X by another name`.
- **§44 The unclaimed void.** Replace a sweeping negative with what was actually checked and what it showed (`none of the three guides covers drivers' hours`). Avoid `the industry has no answer`, `nobody is talking about this`, `there is no framework for`.
- **§45 The unnamed referent.** Name the thing. Avoid `the part that ...`, `the thing you ...`, `the piece nobody ...`, `the bit where ...`, `is the clearest sign that ...`, `not yours to ...`.
- **§46 The assigned memory.** Never tell the reader what their past holds. Offer recognition as a condition (`If you have ever ...`) or a modal (`you may have seen ...`), with an indefinite article for the scene. Avoid `You have sat in the meeting where`, `You've watched`, `We have all seen`, `Everyone has`.
- **§47 The descriptive epithet.** Use the name the text already has. Avoid a description built from other names joined by a verb (`the firm Kestrel bought its scanners from`): it makes the reader solve it, and it asserts a relation nobody sourced. Give a sourced relation its own clause and its source.
- **§48 The stand-in character.** State a claim about a type of person in one clause and give the mechanism plainly. Avoid the invented representative with a story (`The new driver who questions the route, and is overruled twice, stops asking`), a comparison with no baseline (`than before`, `than ever`), and a reason given as a figure (`because the system has spoken`). Use the first person when the argument is yours.
- **§49 The elliptical punchline.** Keep every word a line needs to be read once, and state the conclusion in the verb. In headings, subtitles and teasers especially, never cut a clause short (`how often` with nothing after it) or carry a noun over to a different verb.
- **§50 A title and subtitle that carry the argument.** Give each line one job. The title names the subject and states at most one claim, within 75 characters and one sentence. The subtitle adds the one piece of context the title lacks, or names the reader, within 120 characters and one sentence. Cut whole clauses to fit.
- **§51 Literature-review order.** Open each section on its point, then why it holds, then who showed it. Avoid opening on `According to`, a date, a year, or a study or author as the subject.
- **§52 The empty announcement.** Put the claim in the sentence that would have announced it. Avoid `The figures show what that change is worth.`, `This is where it gets interesting.`, `That is worth pausing on.`, and instructions that count unnamed objects (`check two documents`).
- **§53 The bare aphorism.** Give each abstraction an owner and an object (`The depot's volume forecast does not commit the carrier to any number of vans`). Avoid the short `An X is not a Y.` that stops there.
- **§54 The orphaned reference.** Name a set where you point at it. Write `those five answers` or `that setting` only when the five answers or the setting are named in the same or the previous paragraph.
- **§55 The nominalised claim.** Let the actor do the verb (`The board understood what had gone wrong`). Avoid a noun propped up by a wh-clause (`the board's understanding of what went wrong`) and any subject that runs past six words before its verb.
- **§56 The audit-trail sentence.** One job per sentence, point first. Give an event its date and nothing else before the subject; give a gloss its own clause; let one source marker carry the custody of a quotation. Avoid stacking a date, an interval, a role and a name before the verb. Cut a computed detail the sentence never uses.
- **§57 The quoted label.** Say what the speaker claimed, in plain words, and mark it as a paraphrase. Quote only words that are evidence in themselves: an admission, a commitment, a figure, a definition the argument turns on. Avoid quoting a speaker's figure of speech (the "heartbeat", the "crown jewels") as if it proved something.
- **§58 The compliance clause.** Name the concrete case, or give the dependency its own subject (`if other people rely on what you run, such as ...`). Avoid audience definitions such as `anyone who operates a system that others rely on`.
- **§59 The private code word.** Say the plain thing the first time and every time. Keep at most one image, glossed where it stands, and never reuse it later as a label for what it illustrated. Headings name subjects; they are not figures.

## Before handing the text to humanise

The scanner tests most of the entries above. These need a reading, so make them yourself before the hand-over:

1. Every point, figure, date, source and placeholder you settled at the start is in the text.
2. One name per thing throughout (§11), and every actor visible where the reader needs it (§13).
3. Every set of three or more members varies its construction (§39), and every run of findings keeps its reasoning (§41).
4. Every caption and alt text reads the picture before it gives the values (§40).
5. Every description that stands where a name could go uses the name (§45, §47), and every generic person is a claim stated plainly (§48).
6. Read the headings on their own: each names its subject (§36, §37, §59).
